\begin{abstract}

pyvoa (Python Virus Open Analysis) is an open-source Python library providing
access to 23 epidemiological data resources through a single query with five keywords. They range from global aggregators, such as Our World in Data, to
national agencies, and cover COVID-19, mpox, measles, and Ebola. Each resource
is described by a JSON file rather than by code, so the catalogue can be
extended without modifying the library. Resources are normalised into a common
pandas DataFrame, and a geolocation layer reconciles location identifiers and
provides geometries.  Data are read by default from the project's Zenodo archive, so that series
remain accessible after providers stop publishing, or directly from live
sources for ongoing outbreaks. Charts and maps can be produced in a few lines of Python. pyvoa is MIT-licensed and available from PyPI.

\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}
\label{sec:motivation}

The COVID-19 pandemic produced open epidemiological data at an unprecedented
scale. Global aggregators such as the Johns Hopkins University Center for
Systems Science and Engineering (JHU CSSE)~\cite{dong2020,dong2022} and Our
World in Data (OWID)~\cite{mathieu2021}, as well as national agencies such as
Santé publique France, the German Robert Koch-Institut, and the Italian
Dipartimento della Protezione Civile, published frequently updated series
online. These data, however, were released using heterogeneous file formats,
column vocabularies, temporal granularities and, above all, incompatible
geographic identifiers.

Two major challenges limit the reuse of these data: heterogeneity and
impermanence.

\bigskip

\textbf{Heterogeneity.} Datasets from different providers cannot generally be
compared directly. Equivalent indicators carry different column names from one
source to another, dates follow different formats, daily and cumulative
quantities appear under similar labels, country names vary
(\texttt{United States} / \texttt{US} / \texttt{USA}), and sub-national units
are identified sometimes by name and sometimes by code. 
Reporting schedules also differ: pauses over weekends or holidays can produce
artificial spikes when publication resumes~\cite{dong2022}. Comparative analysis therefore requires
source-specific reconciliation procedures, often implemented separately for
each study and not always made available alongside the resulting analysis. This
process is both time-consuming and error-prone. Heterogeneity also raises the
technical barrier for audiences to whom these data are particularly valuable:
secondary-school and university students, science journalists, and researchers
from other disciplines.



\bigskip

\textbf{Impermanence.} Open data are not necessarily permanent data. The JHU
CSSE team documented the substantial effort required to maintain access to
pandemic data~\cite{dong2022}: web scrapers were developed for more than four
hundred sources, each requiring maintenance whenever a provider changed the
structure of its website. Availability also changed over time. Some providers reduced the geographic resolution of their reporting; others
shifted from daily to weekly updates or adopted an irregular schedule. In other cases,
datasets themselves disappeared. The Chilean Ministry of Science, for
instance, published national COVID-19 data in a public GitHub repository that
is no longer publicly accessible~\cite{minciencia}.
Retrospective and comparative analyses depend on datasets that are sometimes
no longer available at their original locations and therefore cannot always be
independently retrieved by a reviewer. Long-term data accessibility has thus
become a key requirement for reproducibility.

\bigskip

Dashboards were an early response to the demand for accessible pandemic
information. A survey conducted in 2020 initially identified 265 public
web-based COVID-19 dashboards, of which 158 were ultimately included in its
comparative assessment~\cite{ivankovic2021}. Dashboards, however, are primarily
designed to display information rather than to provide reusable data. Their
choice of indicators, geographic coverage, and temporal scope is determined by
their authors; the underlying datasets may not be available for download; and
the displayed information cannot be readily recombined with data
from other sources.

\bigskip

Several software tools have also been developed to facilitate access to pandemic data. \texttt{covid}~\cite{covid_pypi} and
\texttt{COVID19Py}~\cite{covid19py} provide lightweight interfaces to a small
number of original data sources, but offer limited support for analysis and
visualisation. The COVIDcast Epidata API~\cite{reinhart2021} offers a
well-documented collection of more than 170 signals from 12 data sources,
combining traditional public health indicators with auxiliary signals derived
from medical claims, surveys, mobility data, and internet search trends. These
signals are provided in a common format at fine geographic and temporal
resolution, and historical revisions are explicitly tracked. Its geographic
coverage, however, remains focused on the United States.
\texttt{covid19.analytics}~\cite{ponce2021} gives access within the R
ecosystem to several sources of global COVID-19 data, including case, testing,
vaccination, and genomic data. It also includes data-integrity checks,
analytical and modelling tools, and static and interactive visualisations.
Replicas of live datasets are maintained in a dedicated GitHub backup
repository for redundancy and robust accessibility.


\texttt{pyvoa} was designed to combine data access, geographic reconciliation,
visualisation, and data preservation within a single Python framework.
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarises the main differences between
\texttt{pyvoa} and the tools discussed above.

pyvoa addresses both challenges within a single architecture, described in
Section~\ref{sec:description}.


\bigskip

This architecture is not specific to COVID-19. The catalogue has progressively
been extended to other infectious diseases and surveillance systems, including
mpox~\cite{owid_mpox}, measles cases in the United
States~\cite{ahmadi2025}, and the 2026 Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the
Democratic Republic of the Congo~\cite{bdbv2026}. These resources are described using the same JSON-based mechanism as their
COVID-19 counterparts. New resources can therefore be added without modifying
the core library.

\section{Software description}
\label{sec:description}

\subsection{Software architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

pyvoa starts from the open data that providers publish on the web and ends with
a small set of functions called by the user. Between the two, the data follow a
single path (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). The content served by a provider is
in most cases deposited on Zenodo, which gives a citable, long-term archive
hosted at CERN. A preprocessing stage then turns each heterogeneous data
resource into a common internal representation. A unified Python interface
finally returns time series, histograms, maps or exported tables. Preprocessing
forms the back end and the interface the front end; the user only ever calls
the front end and never handles a URL.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figures/schema_PYVOA_vertical.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of pyvoa. Open data from the providers are in most cases
archived on Zenodo. The preprocessing stage reads each data resource through
its JSON descriptor and normalises it; the unified Python interface returns
time series, histograms, maps and exported tables.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Back end}

\textbf{Curation (manual).} Adding a data resource takes two steps, neither of
which involves writing Python. First, the content served at the provider's URL
can be deposited in the pyvoa community on Zenodo~\cite{zenodo_pyvoa}. Second, the resource is described in a JSON file stored
in \texttt{pyvoa/data/}, in the GitHub repository. This descriptor gives the
two locations of the data: the Zenodo mirror, if there is one (\texttt{urldata}), and the
provider's live URL (\texttt{urlparent}). It also gives the separator and
decimal conventions, the columns to drop and the rows to select. It maps each
of the provider's column names onto pyvoa's own vocabulary. Lastly, it gives the geographic granularity
(\texttt{country}, \texttt{region} or \texttt{subregion}) and the ISO~3166-1
alpha-3 code of the territory covered. Of the 23
data resources, 19 can currently be read from both locations, 3 from the
archive only, their provider having withdrawn the files, and 1 from its
provider only, its publisher asking to be consulted before any republication;
the repository README lists the status and the licence of each. 


\textbf{Preprocessing (automatic).} When a data resource is loaded,
\texttt{MetaInfo} and \texttt{DataParser} read its descriptor, fetch the data
and reshape them into a normalised table. The data are read from the Zenodo
mirror when one exists, and from the provider otherwise; the reference tables
of the geographic layer (geometries, populations, composition of the
groupings) are always read from Zenodo. Data read from the archive are
therefore reproducible: a given release of pyvoa always returns the same
numbers. Locations are then mapped onto an internal geographic abstraction
layer, so that geographic identifiers do not depend on the data resource. For
international data this layer follows the United Nations M49
standard~\cite{geoscheme}; for sub-national data it follows the administrative
nomenclature of the country concerned. \texttt{GeoManager} converts between
the \texttt{iso2}, \texttt{iso3}, \texttt{num} and \texttt{name} codings.
Country names are resolved with pycountry~\cite{pycountry}, preceded by an
alias table for spellings specific to some providers, so that
\texttt{"Korea, South"} (JHU) and \texttt{"South Korea"} (OWID) designate the
same country; accents and case are ignored, so that \texttt{"cote d'ivoire"}
and \texttt{"Côte d'Ivoire"} resolve alike. \texttt{GeoRegion} resolves
supranational groupings (M49 regions, European Union, G20, OECD and others),
so that a grouping can be passed as a single \texttt{where} argument.
\texttt{GeoCountry} provides sub-national units with their geometries and
populations; for France and the United States, variants place the overseas
territories next to the mainland or omit them. Finally, \texttt{GPDBuilder}
joins the table to the geometries, yielding a single geopandas
\texttt{GeoDataFrame}~\cite{geopandas} held internally.


\subsubsection{Front end}

The user interacts with a single module, \texttt{pyvoa.front}, conventionally
imported as \texttt{pf}. On import, the module creates one instance of its
interface class and exposes its methods as plain functions, so that
\texttt{pf.setwhom('owid')} selects a data resource without any object being
created by the user. That instance keeps the state of the session (selected
resource, archive or live mode, chart backend) from one call to the next.


\subsection{Software functionalities}
\label{sec:functionalities}

\textbf{Selecting data.} A session starts with \texttt{pf.setwhom()}, which
selects one of the twenty-three data resources listed by
\texttt{pf.listwhom()}. By default, the data are read from the Zenodo archive
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). For an epidemic still under way,
\texttt{pf.setlive(True)} reads them directly from the providers, which gives
the most recent figures at the expense of reproducibility. Some files are
nevertheless always read from the archive: the geographic reference tables,
and the data of resources no longer available from their providers.
Conversely, a resource without a mirror is always read from its provider
(Section~\ref{sec:ex-beyond}). Downloaded files are cached locally, with
archived and live copies stored separately.

\textbf{One query grammar.} Four functions take the same five keywords:
\texttt{get()} returns the data, while \texttt{plot()}, \texttt{hist()} and
\texttt{map()} draw them. Together, the keywords specify the query:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{where}: a location, a list of locations, or a grouping such as
  \texttt{'G20'} or \texttt{'Western Europe'}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:ex-timeseries} and~\ref{sec:ex-map});
\item \texttt{which}: one of the indicators of the selected resource;
\item \texttt{what}: the value as published (\texttt{'current'}), or its
  day-to-day or week-to-week difference (\texttt{'daily'}, \texttt{'weekly'});
\item \texttt{when}: a date (\texttt{'dd/mm/yyyy'}) or a period in slice-like
  notation (\texttt{'dd/mm/yyyy:dd/mm/yyyy'}), either bound being optional;
\item \texttt{option}: transformations such as a seven-day rolling mean
  (\texttt{'smooth7'}), the sum over the selected locations
  (\texttt{'sumall'}), or a normalisation by population, per inhabitant up to
  per million (\texttt{'normalize:pop'} to \texttt{'normalize:pop1M'};
  Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}).
\end{itemize}


\textbf{Outputs.} \texttt{get()} returns a table with one row per date and
location: by default a geopandas \texttt{GeoDataFrame}, which carries the
geometry of each location, or on request a plain pandas \texttt{DataFrame}
(Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}). Both are ordinary pandas objects, so further
analysis and export rely on the usual pandas methods. The same rows can also
be returned, without geometry, as a list, a dictionary or a NumPy array.

\textbf{Charts.} Charts are drawn with matplotlib~\cite{hunter2007} or
bokeh~\cite{bokeh}, both installed with the package and chosen with
\texttt{pf.setvis()}. \texttt{plot()} draws an indicator against time
(Section~\ref{sec:ex-timeseries}), two indicators against each other, or one
curve per year over the calendar, so that seasons can be compared; \texttt{hist()} draws bar charts comparing locations (Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}), histograms of the values, or pie
charts (Section~\ref{sec:ex-beyond}); \texttt{map()} draws choropleth maps
(Section~\ref{sec:ex-map}), on an equal-area projection with matplotlib.
Titles and legends are the same with both backends. With matplotlib,
\texttt{pf.savefig()} writes an image file; with bokeh, it writes an
interactive HTML page, in which maps, bar charts and pie charts can carry a
date slider.


\textbf{Discovering the vocabulary.} Each keyword has a function, named after
it, that lists the values it accepts: \texttt{listwhom()},
\texttt{listwhich()}, \texttt{listwhere()}, \texttt{listwhat()},
\texttt{listoption()}, and \texttt{listvis()}, \texttt{listplot()},
\texttt{listhist()} or \texttt{listmap()} for the charts. Each returns a plain
Python list; \texttt{listwhich()} and \texttt{listwhere()} answer for the
selected resource. \texttt{getwhichinfo()} prints the definition of an indicator and the file it is read from, and \texttt{whattodo()} gathers every keyword and its
values in a single table. A user can thus explore a resource without
consulting the documentation, which matters for teaching
(Section~\ref{sec:impact}).


\subsection{Development history and quality assurance}
\label{sec:history}

\textbf{History.} pyvoa is the third name of one continuous project, led
throughout by the same three authors. It began as \emph{CoCoA} (Covid
Collaborative Analysis) during the first COVID-19 lockdown in France, at the
Data Against COVID-19 hackathon of April 2020, with the aim it still has:
unified access to pandemic data for people who are not data specialists.
Renamed \emph{PyCoA} (Python Covid Analysis) in November 2020, it acquired
support for several databases and sub-national geography in 2021,
normalisation by population in 2022, and JSON descriptors in 2023, over some
1900 commits recorded in the project's changelog. The same year it was
renamed \emph{pyvoa}, Python Virus Open Analysis, as its scope widened beyond
COVID-19. pyvoa was developed in a branch of the PyCoA repository until March
2025, then in a repository of its own. Version 1.0.0 (November 2026),
described here, is its first stable release.

\textbf{Quality assurance.} An offline test suite covers the parsing of the
descriptors, the geographic layer and the visualisation layer; tests that
need a network connection are marked and excluded from it. Continuous
integration on GitHub Actions runs this suite on Linux with Python 3.10 to
3.14, on macOS and Windows with the oldest and the newest of these versions,
and with every dependency at the minimum version declared in
Table~\ref{codeMetadata}. It also checks the code style, the documentation
build, and the consistency of this manuscript with the code. A weekly job
runs the network tests, which compare every data resource, as parsed by
pyvoa, with an independent reading of its source files.

\textbf{Release and citation.} Releases are published on PyPI and archived on
Zenodo, each with its own DOI, all of them grouped under a concept DOI. The
git history is also preserved by Software Heritage, which identifies each
release by an intrinsic identifier (SWHID), computed from its content and
therefore independent of where the code is hosted. The repository carries
citation and software metadata (\texttt{CITATION.cff},
\texttt{codemeta.json}), contribution and conduct guidelines, and a changelog
covering every release since 0.1.0 as well as the CoCoA and PyCoA years. For
the PyCoA period, whose repository is private, this changelog is the only
public record.

\textbf{Use of generative AI.} Generative AI was used at two stages of the
development. For release 0.3.1 (July 2025), the docstrings were drafted with
large language model assistants (GitHub Copilot, Claude). In preparing
release 1.0.0, the authors used Claude Code (Anthropic) to locate and fix
defects, write regression tests, check every data resource against its
source files, maintain the metadata and package the software. Every change
was reviewed by the authors before being committed.

\section{Illustrative examples}
\label{sec:examples}

The four examples below are taken from the notebooks and scripts of the
repository (\texttt{examples/}); run in sequence after
\texttt{pip install pyvoa}, each produces the output shown.

\subsection{Comparing countries through time series}
\label{sec:ex-timeseries}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import pyvoa.front as pf
pf.setwhom('owid')            # Our World in Data, worldwide, by country
pf.setvis('matplotlib')
pf.plot(which='total_deaths', where='Western Europe',
        what='daily', option='smooth7')
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{where='Western Europe'} is expanded by \texttt{GeoRegion} into the nine
states of the corresponding United Nations geoscheme subregion (standard
M49)~\cite{geoscheme}; \texttt{what='daily'} takes the day-to-day difference of
the cumulative series the source publishes; and \texttt{option='smooth7'}
applies a seven-day rolling mean, which suppresses the weekday reporting
artefacts common in national series (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig2_timeseries_weu.pdf}
\caption{Daily COVID-19 deaths in the nine states of Western Europe (M49),
seven-day smoothed, from Our World in Data.}
\label{fig:timeseries}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Mapping a country grouping}
\label{sec:ex-map}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('jhu')             # Johns Hopkins CSSE, worldwide
pf.map(which='tot_confirmed', where='G20', when='31/12/2021')
\end{lstlisting}

As in the previous example, \texttt{where} names a grouping of countries
rather than listing them, here the nineteen member countries of the
G20~\cite{g20}. The geolocation layer then supplies their geometries, so
that the map is drawn without any further input; countries outside the
grouping are simply left uncoloured. Since no \texttt{what} keyword is
given, each country is shown with the value its source publishes, here the
cumulative number of confirmed cases. This series is no longer updated, the
upstream collection having ended in March~2023, but it remains available
from its Zenodo mirror, which is where pyvoa reads it (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig3_map_g20.pdf}
\caption{Total confirmed COVID-19 cases in the nineteen member countries of
the G20 on 31~December 2021, from the Johns Hopkins CSSE mirror. Map lines delineate study areas and do not necessarily depict accepted national boundaries.}
\label{fig:map}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Sub-national data, normalised by population}
\label{sec:ex-hist}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('spf')    # Sante Publique France db
pf.hist(which='cur_hosp', when='31/12/2021',
        option='normalize:pop1M')
\end{lstlisting}

The same grammar addresses a French source at \emph{département} granularity.
It also introduces the \texttt{option} keyword. Here,
\texttt{option='normalize:pop1M'} divides the count by the population that the
geolocation layer carries, turning patient numbers into a rate per million
inhabitants, so that \emph{départements} of very different sizes can be
compared. By default, \texttt{hist()} ranks the bars by value and draws the
twelve highest (Fig.~\ref{fig:hist}).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig4_hist_spf.pdf}
\caption{Patients hospitalised for COVID-19 per million inhabitants on
31~December 2021, in the twelve French \emph{départements} with the highest
rate, from Santé publique France.}
\label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

Called with the same keywords, \texttt{pf.get()} returns the data themselves
rather than a picture: every \emph{département}, as a
\texttt{GeoDataFrame}~\cite{geopandas}, that is, a pandas
frame~\cite{mckinney2010} with a geometry column. pyvoa is thus meant to be
the starting point of an analysis, not its boundary.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('spf')    # Sante Publique France db
pdf = pf.get(which='cur_hosp', when='31/12/2021',
             option='normalize:pop1M')
\end{lstlisting}

value, and five columns: the date, the location, its code, the requested

\subsection{Beyond COVID-19}
\label{sec:ex-beyond}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('ebolardc')
pf.hist(which='tot_confirmed', typeofhist='pie', when='01/10/2026')
\end{lstlisting}


Moving to another disease requires no new syntax: only the resource changes.
The Institut National de Santé Publique of the Democratic Republic of the
Congo indexes its situation reports on the Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak by
\emph{zone de santé} (health zone), a geography that the geolocation layer
resolves just as it resolves French \emph{départements} or US states. With
\texttt{typeofhist='pie'}, \texttt{hist()} shows a composition rather than a
ranking, and gathers the smaller zones into a single slice instead of
discarding them (Fig.~\ref{fig:beyond}). Unlike the other examples, these
data are not archived: the Institut asks to be consulted before any
republication, so pyvoa always reads them from the provider. The
\texttt{when} keyword sets the date shown, but a revision of past reports by
the Institut could still change the values.

\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/fig5_ebola_drc.pdf}
\caption{Confirmed cases of the 2026 Bundibugyo
ebolavirus outbreak by health zone of the Democratic Republic of the Congo,
from the situation reports of the Institut National de Santé Publique, on
1~October 2026.}
\label{fig:beyond}
\end{figure}

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}

\textbf{Cross-source comparison.} Because the twenty-three data resources
share one schema, series from unrelated providers can be put side by side
without any source-specific parsing. Fig.~\ref{fig:wastewater} compares the
SARS-CoV-2 signal in French wastewater (\texttt{sumeau}) with the confirmed
cases and hospital admissions reported by Santé publique France
(\texttt{spfnational}), using five pyvoa calls and a few lines of pandas. The
cases move with the wastewater signal, and the admissions follow it by about
a week, consistent with the lead over emergency visits reported by the
SUM'Eau network~\cite{jourdain2025}. In 2023 the cases fall away as testing
declines: the comparison brings out this reporting artefact instead of
leaving it hidden.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig6_wastewater_spf.pdf}
\caption{Left: SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater (SUM'Eau), and confirmed cases and
hospital admissions in France, as weekly means scaled to their maximum, from
August 2022 to June 2023. Right: correlation of cases and of admissions with
the wastewater signal as a function of the lag; a positive lag means that the
series follows the wastewater signal.}
\label{fig:wastewater}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Data archive and reproducibility.} pyvoa makes retrospective work
possible on series whose providers have stopped publishing. The JHU series of
Section~\ref{sec:ex-map} is no longer updated, and the Chilean data cited in
Section~\ref{sec:motivation} are no longer available from their provider at
all; both are still retrieved in one line, with \texttt{pf.setwhom('jhu')}
and \texttt{pf.setwhom('minciencia')}. The archive also makes analyses reproducible: a pyvoa script records every
choice made in a few short calls, and, for archived resources, returns the
same numbers when it is run again years later
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}).

\textbf{Teaching and outreach.} pyvoa has been used since 2020 in a physics
and data analysis course at Université Paris Cité, and was designed with this
use in mind: a complete figure takes a few lines, and every accepted value
can be listed at run time. Students thus work with real data, heterogeneous
and incomplete, from the first session rather than with a cleaned teaching
set. Since \texttt{get()} returns a pandas frame, they can then move from a
first figure to an analysis of their own without changing tool. The same
features make pyvoa accessible to the general public, to science journalists
and to researchers from other disciplines. It was presented in Paris, under its earlier name PyCoA, at the Salon Culture et Jeux Mathématiques and at the Fête de la Science from 2021 to 2023.

\textbf{Adoption.} Under its earlier name, PyCoA, the software was featured
by the CNRS MODCOV19 modelling platform~\cite{modcov19}. Its data.gouv.fr
page, which lists pyvoa as a reuse of French public data, has been viewed
more than 6\,000 times since July 2022~\cite{datagouv_pyvoa}.
Since its first release on PyPI in December 2024,
it has been installed about 420 times with pip or uv outside
continuous-integration environments, according to the public PyPI download
logs (\texttt{bigquery-public-data.pypi.file\_downloads}); mirrors and other
automated clients, which account for most of the 10\,122 recorded downloads,
are excluded. This is a lower bound, since installations from the GitHub
sources are not recorded. Over the same period, COVID19Py~\cite{covid19py},

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

pyvoa brings twenty-three epidemiological data resources, from global
aggregators to national agencies, under one Python interface. Each resource
is described by a JSON file, normalised into a common table and placed on a
shared geography, so that series from different providers can be compared
directly. Resources with a Zenodo mirror remain available after their
provider stops publishing, and analyses based on them can be run again
unchanged. 

The pandemic's open data are now a historical record, and an increasingly
fragile one, while the diseases that followed produce their own. Future work
follows from this: extending the catalogue to further diseases, refining the
sub-national geography, and archiving new resources before they disappear.
Contributions, in particular new resource descriptors, are welcome through
the repository~\cite{pyvoa_github}, which documents the procedure.

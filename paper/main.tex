\begin{abstract}

pyvoa (Python Virus Open Analysis) is an open-source Python library providing
access to 23 epidemiological data resources through a single query with five keywords. They range from global aggregators, such as Our World in Data, to
national agencies, and cover COVID-19, mpox, measles, and Ebola. Each resource
is described by a JSON file rather than by code, so the catalogue can be
extended without modifying the library. Resources are normalised into a common
pandas DataFrame, and a geolocation layer reconciles location identifiers and
provides geometries. Data are read from the project's Zenodo archive by
default, or directly from live sources for ongoing outbreaks, so series remain accessible after providers stop publishing. Charts and maps can be produced in a few lines of Python. pyvoa is MIT-licensed and available from PyPI.

\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}
\label{sec:motivation}

The COVID-19 pandemic produced open epidemiological data at an unprecedented
scale. Global aggregators such as the Johns Hopkins University Center for
Systems Science and Engineering (JHU CSSE)~\cite{dong2020,dong2022} and Our
World in Data (OWID)~\cite{mathieu2021}, as well as national agencies such as
Santé publique France, the German Robert Koch-Institut, and the Italian
Dipartimento della Protezione Civile, published frequently updated series
online. These data, however, were released using heterogeneous file formats,
column vocabularies, temporal granularities and, above all, incompatible
geographic identifiers.

Two major challenges limit the reuse of these data: heterogeneity and
impermanence.

\bigskip

\textbf{Heterogeneity.} Datasets from different providers cannot generally be
compared directly. Equivalent indicators carry different column names from one
source to another, dates follow different formats, daily and cumulative
quantities appear under similar labels, country names vary
(\texttt{United States} / \texttt{US} / \texttt{USA}), and sub-national units
are identified sometimes by name and sometimes by code. 
Reporting schedules also differ: pauses over weekends or holidays can produce
artificial spikes when publication resumes~\cite{dong2022}. Comparative analysis therefore requires
source-specific reconciliation procedures, often implemented separately for
each study and not always made available alongside the resulting analysis. This
process is both time-consuming and error-prone. Heterogeneity also raises the
technical barrier for audiences to whom these data are particularly valuable:
secondary-school and university students, science journalists, and researchers
from other disciplines.



\bigskip

\textbf{Impermanence.} Open data are not necessarily permanent data. The JHU
CSSE team documented the substantial effort required to maintain access to
pandemic data~\cite{dong2022}: web scrapers were developed for more than four
hundred sources, each requiring maintenance whenever a provider changed the
structure of its website. Availability also changed over time. Some providers reduced the geographic resolution of their reporting; others
shifted from daily to weekly updates or adopted an irregular schedule. In other cases,
datasets themselves disappeared. The Chilean Ministry of Science, for
instance, published national COVID-19 data in a public GitHub repository that
is no longer publicly accessible~\cite{minciencia}.
Retrospective and comparative analyses depend on datasets that are sometimes
no longer available at their original locations and therefore cannot always be
independently retrieved by a reviewer. Long-term data accessibility has thus
become a key requirement for reproducibility.

\bigskip

Dashboards were an early response to the demand for accessible pandemic
information. A survey conducted in 2020 initially identified 265 public
web-based COVID-19 dashboards, of which 158 were ultimately included in its
comparative assessment~\cite{ivankovic2021}. Dashboards, however, are primarily
designed to display information rather than to provide reusable data. Their
choice of indicators, geographic coverage, and temporal scope is determined by
their authors; the underlying datasets may not be available for download; and
the displayed information cannot be readily recombined with data
from other sources.

\bigskip

Several software tools have also been developed to facilitate access to pandemic data. \texttt{covid}~\cite{covid_pypi} and
\texttt{COVID19Py}~\cite{covid19py} provide lightweight interfaces to a small
number of original data sources, but offer limited support for analysis and
visualisation.  The COVIDcast Epidata API~\cite{reinhart2021} offers a
well-documented collection of more than 170 signals from 12 data sources,
combining traditional public health indicators with auxiliary signals derived
from medical claims, surveys, mobility data, and internet search trends. These
signals are provided in a common format at fine geographic and temporal
resolution, and historical revisions are explicitly tracked. Its geographic
coverage, however, remains focused on the United States.
\texttt{covid19.analytics}~\cite{ponce2021} gives access within the R
ecosystem to several sources of global COVID-19 data, including case, testing,
vaccination, and genomic data. It also includes data-integrity checks,
analytical and modelling tools, and static and interactive visualisations.
Replicas of live datasets are maintained in a dedicated GitHub backup
repository for redundancy and robust accessibility.


\texttt{pyvoa} was designed to combine data access, geographic reconciliation,
visualisation, and data preservation within a single Python framework.
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarises the main differences between
\texttt{pyvoa} and the tools discussed above.

The two challenges identified above are addressed in \texttt{pyvoa} through a
common architecture. Twenty-three data resources are described declaratively,
normalised into a common schema, and linked to a geographic layer that
reconciles location identifiers and provides the corresponding geometries.
Data can be retrieved either from their original sources, when current values
are required, or from the project's Zenodo deposits, which preserve previously
retrieved datasets. Once a data resource has been integrated, the same interface can
be used for data retrieval, geographic processing, and visualisation, allowing
a complete figure to be produced in only a few lines of Python.


\bigskip

This architecture is not specific to COVID-19. The catalogue has progressively
been extended to other infectious diseases and surveillance systems, including
mpox~\cite{owid_mpox}, measles cases in the United
States~\cite{ahmadi2025}, and the 2026 Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the
Democratic Republic of the Congo~\cite{bdbv2026}. These resources are described using the same JSON-based mechanism as their
COVID-19 counterparts. New resources can therefore be added without modifying
the core library.


\bigskip

The archival component complements this source-agnostic architecture by
preserving retrieved datasets as persistent and independently citable records.
Archived copies can therefore remain accessible even if the original source
changes or disappears, improving both recoverability and reproducibility.

\section{Software description}
\label{sec:description}

\subsection{Software architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

pyvoa starts from the open data that providers publish on the web and ends with
a small set of functions called by the user. Between the two, the data follow a
single path (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). The content served by a provider is
in most cases deposited on Zenodo, which gives a citable, long-term archive
hosted at CERN. A preprocessing stage then turns each heterogeneous data
resource into a common internal representation. A unified Python interface
finally returns time series, histograms, maps or exported tables. Preprocessing
forms the back end and the interface the front end; the user only ever calls
the front end and never handles a URL.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/schema_PYVOA_vertical.pdf}
\caption{Architecture of pyvoa. Open data from the providers are in most cases
archived on Zenodo. The preprocessing stage reads each data resource through
its JSON descriptor and normalises it; the unified Python interface returns
time series, histograms, maps and exported tables.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Back end}

\textbf{Curation (manual).} Adding a data resource takes two steps, neither of
which involves writing Python. First, the content served at the provider's URL
can be deposited in the pyvoa community on Zenodo~\cite{zenodo_pyvoa}. Second, the resource is described in a JSON file stored
in \texttt{pyvoa/data/}, in the GitHub repository. This descriptor gives the
two locations of the data: the Zenodo mirror (\texttt{urldata}) (if it exists) and the
provider's live URL (\texttt{urlparent}). It also gives the separator and
decimal conventions, the columns to drop and the rows to select. It maps each
of the provider's column names onto pyvoa's own vocabulary. Lastly, it gives the geographic granularity
(\texttt{country}, \texttt{region} or \texttt{subregion}) and the ISO~3166-1
alpha-3 code of the territory covered.  Of the 23
data resources, 19 can currently be read from both locations, 3 from the
archive only, their provider having withdrawn the files, and 1 from its
provider only, its publisher asking to be consulted before any republication;
the repository README lists the status and the licence of each. Licences are
the providers': most are open, one rules out commercial reuse, and two
compilations rest on official data published with no licence or reserved
before republication.

\textbf{Preprocessing (automatic).} On loading, \texttt{MetaInfo} and
\texttt{DataParser} read the descriptor, fetch the data and reshape them into a
normalised table. By default the data are read from the Zenodo mirror, so that
a given release of pyvoa always returns the same numbers; a resource without a
mirror is read from its provider. Locations are then mapped onto an internal
geographic abstraction layer, so that changing data resource leaves geographic
identifiers unchanged. For international data this layer follows the United
Nations M49 standard~\cite{geoscheme}; for sub-national data it follows the
administrative nomenclature in use in the country concerned.
\texttt{GeoManager} converts between the \texttt{iso2}, \texttt{iso3},
\texttt{name} and \texttt{num} standards and absorbs the spellings specific to
each provider: names are stripped of accents and case-folded, so that
\texttt{"cote d'ivoire"} and \texttt{"Côte d'Ivoire"} resolve alike.
\texttt{GeoRegion} resolves supranational groupings (M49 regions, European
Union, G20, OECD and others), so that a grouping is a single \texttt{where}
argument. \texttt{GeoCountry} provides sub-national units and their geometries,
with variants that draw overseas territories in place, next to the mainland, or
not at all. Finally, \texttt{GPDBuilder} joins the table to the geometries. The
result is held internally as a single geopandas
\texttt{GeoDataFrame}~\cite{geopandas}. The geographic layer can be imported on
its own and is also useful outside epidemiology.

\subsubsection{Front end}

The user interacts with a single module, \texttt{pyvoa.front}, conventionally
imported as \texttt{pf}. It is used through plain function calls, such as
\texttt{pf.setwhom('owid')} to select a data resource: there is no class to
instantiate, and the module keeps the state of the session (selected resource,
archive or live mode, chart backend) between calls. Its functions are presented
in Section~\ref{sec:functionalities}.

\subsection{Software functionalities}
\label{sec:functionalities}

\textbf{Selecting data.} A session starts by choosing a data resource with
\texttt{pf.setwhom()}; \texttt{pf.listwhom()} lists the twenty-three available.
By default the data are read from the Zenodo archive, so that an analysis
returns the same numbers whenever it is run again. \texttt{pf.setlive(True)}
reads the providers' current files instead, which an epidemic still under way
requires, at the cost of that reproducibility (Section~\ref{sec:ex-beyond}).
Downloaded files are cached locally, the archived and the live copies apart.

\textbf{One query grammar.} Four functions share the same keywords:
\texttt{get()} returns the data, and \texttt{plot()}, \texttt{hist()} and
\texttt{map()} draw them. The keywords state what is asked for:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{where}: a location, a list of locations, or a grouping such as
  \texttt{'G20'} or \texttt{'Western Europe'}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:ex-timeseries} and~\ref{sec:ex-map});
\item \texttt{which}: the indicator, among those of the selected resource;
\item \texttt{what}: the value as published (\texttt{current}), or its daily
  or weekly change;
\item \texttt{when}: a date or a period;
\item \texttt{option}: transformations such as a seven-day smoothing, the sum
  over the selected locations, or a normalisation by population
  (Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Outputs.} \texttt{get()} returns a table with one row per date and
location: by default a geopandas \texttt{GeoDataFrame}, which carries the
geometry of each location, or on request a pandas \texttt{DataFrame}, a list, a
dictionary or an array (Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}). These are standard Python
objects, so further analysis and export use the usual pandas methods.
Conversely, a user's own table can be passed through the \texttt{input}
keyword, and pyvoa then processes and draws data it did not fetch.

\textbf{Charts.} Charts are drawn with matplotlib~\cite{hunter2007} or
bokeh~\cite{bokeh}, chosen with \texttt{pf.setvis()}. \texttt{plot()} draws
time series (Section~\ref{sec:ex-timeseries}); \texttt{hist()} draws bar charts
comparing locations (Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}), histograms of the values, or
pie charts (Section~\ref{sec:ex-beyond}); \texttt{map()} draws choropleth maps
(Section~\ref{sec:ex-map}), on an equal-area projection with matplotlib and
with a date slider with bokeh. Titles, legends and the export to image files
(\texttt{pf.savefig()}) behave alike with both. The backends are detected at
run time: one that is not installed is reported when it is selected, rather
than breaking the import, and the \texttt{pyvoa-full} distribution installs
them both.

\textbf{Discovering the vocabulary.} Every accepted value can be listed at
run time: \texttt{listwhom()}, \texttt{listwhich()}, \texttt{listwhere()},
\texttt{listwhat()}, \texttt{listoption()} and their counterparts for the
charts return plain Python lists. A user can thus explore a resource without
consulting the documentation, which matters for teaching
(Section~\ref{sec:impact}).

\subsection{Development history and quality assurance}
\label{sec:history}

\textbf{History.} pyvoa is the third name of one continuous project, developed
throughout by the same authors. It began as \emph{CoCoA} (Covid Collaborative
Analysis) at the Data Against COVID-19 hackathon of April 2020, with the aim it
still has: unified access to pandemic data for people who are not data
specialists. Renamed \emph{PyCoA} (Python Covid Analysis) in November 2020, it
gained, over some 1900 commits, its support for several databases and its
sub-national geography in 2021, the normalisation by population in 2022 and the
JSON descriptors in 2023. The same year it became \emph{pyvoa}, Python Virus
Open Analysis, as its scope widened beyond COVID-19. pyvoa was developed in a
branch of the PyCoA repository until March 2025, then in a repository of its
own. Version 0.5.0 (August 2026) is the first release prepared for external
scrutiny.

\textbf{Quality assurance.} An offline test suite covers the parsing of the
descriptors, the geographic layer and the visualisation layer; a test that
needs a network connection must be marked as such, so that the default suite
cannot silently depend on an upstream server. Continuous integration on GitHub
Actions runs the suite on Python 3.10 to 3.14, against both the newest and the
oldest supported version of each dependency; it also checks the code style,
the documentation build, and this manuscript against the code it describes
(database counts, functions used in the listings, dependencies). A weekly job
runs the network tests, which compare every database, as parsed by pyvoa,
with an independent reading of its source files.

\textbf{Release and citation.} Releases are published on PyPI and archived on
Zenodo under a concept DOI; the git history is also preserved by Software
Heritage, which identifies each release by an intrinsic, content-based
identifier (SWHID). The repository carries citation and software
metadata (\texttt{CITATION.cff}, \texttt{codemeta.json}), contribution and
conduct guidelines, and a changelog covering every release since 0.1.0 as well
as the CoCoA and PyCoA years --- the only public record of a history whose
PyCoA repository is private.

\textbf{Use of generative AI.} Generative AI was used at two stages of the development. For release 0.3.1, the
docstrings of the code base were drafted with the help of large language model
assistants (GitHub Copilot, Claude) and reviewed by the authors. In 2026, before
the release described here, an AI coding assistant, Claude (Anthropic) used
through Claude Code, assisted in finalising and debugging the code --- locating
and fixing defects, writing regression tests, and checking every database
against a direct read of its source files --- and in preparing the publication:
maintaining the metadata (\texttt{CITATION.cff}, \texttt{.zenodo.json},
\texttt{codemeta.json}), packaging and releasing on PyPI, and checking the
consistency between the code, its documentation and this article. Every change
was reviewed by the authors before being committed, and is recorded in the
project's changelog.

\section{Illustrative examples}
\label{sec:examples}

The four examples below are taken from the notebooks and scripts shipped with
the package; each runs as printed after \texttt{pip install pyvoa-full}.

\subsection{Comparing countries through time series}
\label{sec:ex-timeseries}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import pyvoa.front as pf
pf.setwhom('owid')            # Our World in Data, worldwide, by country
pf.setvis('matplotlib')
pf.plot(which='total_deaths', where='Western Europe',
        what='daily', option='smooth7')
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{where='Western Europe'} is expanded by \texttt{GeoRegion} into the nine
states of the corresponding United Nations geoscheme subregion (standard
M49)~\cite{geoscheme}; \texttt{what='daily'} takes the day-to-day difference of
the cumulative series the source publishes; and \texttt{option='smooth7'}
applies a seven-day rolling mean, which suppresses the weekday reporting
artefacts common in national series (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig2_timeseries_weu.pdf}
\caption{Daily COVID-19 deaths in the nine
Western European states, seven-day smoothed, from Our World in Data.}
\label{fig:timeseries}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Mapping a country grouping}
\label{sec:ex-map}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('jhu')             # Johns Hopkins CSSE, worldwide
pf.map(which='tot_confirmed', where='G20', when='31/12/2021')
\end{lstlisting}

As in the previous example, \texttt{where} may designate a region rather than a
list of countries, be it geographical, political or economic, provided the
geolocation layer resolves it; here the G20 member states~\cite{g20}, whose
geometries it supplies. Countries outside the grouping are left unfilled, so
the map background shows through. The JHU series is read from its Zenodo
mirror, upstream collection having ended in March~2023
(Fig.~\ref{fig:map}). In the absence of a \texttt{what} keyword, a
\texttt{tot\_} variable is taken at its cumulative value.

\subsection{Sub-national data, normalised by population}
\label{sec:ex-hist}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('spf')    # Sante Publique France db
pf.hist(which='cur_hosp', when='31/12/2021',
        option='normalize:pop1M')
\end{lstlisting}

The same grammar addresses a French source at \emph{département} granularity.
It also introduces the \texttt{option} keyword:
\texttt{option='normalize:pop1M'} divides the count by the population the
geolocation layer carries, turning patient numbers into a rate per million
inhabitants, which is what makes \emph{départements} of very different sizes
comparable; the bars are then ranked by value rather than by name
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hist}).

\begin{figure}[!ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig4_hist_spf.pdf}
\caption{Patients hospitalised for COVID-19 per
million inhabitants by French \emph{département} on 31~December 2021, ranked,
from Santé publique France.}
\label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

The same selection is available as data rather than as a picture. Calling
\texttt{pf.get()} with identical keywords returns a
\texttt{GeoDataFrame}~\cite{geopandas}, that is, a pandas
frame~\cite{mckinney2010} carrying a geometry column: pyvoa is meant to be the
starting point of an analysis, not its boundary.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('spf')    # Sante Publique France db
pdf = pf.get(which='cur_hosp', when='31/12/2021',
             option='normalize:pop1M', what='daily')
\end{lstlisting}

that was asked for and nothing else, under a name that records the
transformations applied to it: \texttt{what} and \texttt{option} each leave

\subsection{Beyond COVID-19}
\label{sec:ex-beyond}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('ebolardc')
pf.hist(which='tot_confirmed', typeofhist='pie', when='01/10/2026')
\end{lstlisting}

The grammar does not change with the disease. The situation reports on
Bundibugyo ebolavirus issued by the Institut National de Santé Publique of the
Democratic Republic of the Congo are indexed by \emph{zone de santé}, a
geography the geolocation layer resolves just as it resolves French
\emph{départements} or United States states; \texttt{typeofhist='pie'} asks for
a composition rather than a ranking, the smaller zones being gathered into a
single slice rather than discarded (Fig.~\ref{fig:beyond}). Measles in the
United States and mpox are read on the same terms. Unlike the other examples,
these data are not archived: the Institut asks to be consulted before any
republication, so pyvoa reads them from the provider at every call, and only
the \texttt{when} keyword keeps the figure from following the latest report.

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}

\textbf{Questions that become tractable.} Two kinds. First, cross-source
comparison. Because twenty-three databases share one schema and one geographic
key, a sub-national national source and an international country-level source
can be put side by side without writing a join: the French wastewater signal and
the reported incidence over the same territory, or a country's own agency series
against the aggregator's rendering of it --- a comparison that surfaces
reporting artefacts rather than assuming them away. Second, retrospective work
on sources that have ceased. The JHU series used in Section~\ref{sec:examples}
is no longer published; it is nonetheless fetched, by identifier, in one line.
The reverse case is served by the same code: an outbreak still under way, such
as the Ebola epidemic of Section~\ref{sec:ex-beyond}, is read from the provider itself, the
archive holding no copy of it.

\textbf{Existing questions pursued better.} The gain is on reproducibility
rather than on speed. An analysis written against pyvoa records which database,
which indicator, which pre-treatment and which options were used, in five
keywords that a reader can re-execute; and because the payload is read from an
archived copy carrying a persistent identifier~\cite{zenodo_pyvoa} rather than
from a live URL, re-execution years later returns the same numbers. This is what
a live endpoint cannot offer, and it is increasingly the limiting factor in
pandemic retrospectives.

\textbf{Daily practice, and who changes it.} The design constraint --- a
complete figure in two lines, every vocabulary enumerable at run time --- was
set for teaching, and the software has been used that way at Université Paris
Cité since 2020, in physics and data analysis courses. Students meet real,
heterogeneous, incomplete data on the first day rather than a cleaned teaching
set; \texttt{get()} then hands them a pandas frame, so the progression from
first figure to independent analysis happens without changing tool. The same
property serves science journalists and researchers from outside epidemiology,
the other audiences the project has targeted from the start.

\textbf{Uptake so far.} The project began at the Data Against COVID-19 hackathon
(April 2020) and was presented at Hackathon COVID --- Lutter Ensemble (April
2021) and at the 22nd Salon Culture et Jeux Mathématiques (May 2021). It was
covered by the CNRS MODCOV19 modelling platform~\cite{modcov19} and in
institutional communications of Sorbonne Université, Université Paris Cité and
LPNHE. Its authors have also registered it, since 2021, as a reuse on
data.gouv.fr, the French national open-data portal~\cite{datagouv_pyvoa}. The
page names the Santé publique France datasets pyvoa reads and the Insee death
records one of its examples uses, so that the software is listed on the page of
each of them, where users of those data look for tools; it shows a short
session in the grammar of Section~\ref{sec:ex-hist}, ending in a map of
hospital deaths per thousand inhabitants by \emph{département}. The page has been viewed over 5\,000 times.
\attn{compléter ici par les usages tiers documentés --- voir note}

\textbf{Reuse, and commercial status.} The geolocation layer is importable on
its own (\texttt{pyvoa.geo}) and contains nothing epidemiological: any dataset
keyed by country, region or French \emph{département} can be reconciled and
mapped with it. Extending the catalogue takes a JSON descriptor and no Python,
which is what makes a new disease a matter of hours rather than of a release
cycle. The software is distributed under the MIT licence, has no commercial
version and is not exploited commercially.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

pyvoa gives one Python interface to twenty-three publicly available
epidemiological databases, most of them openly licensed.
Sources are described declaratively rather than coded, normalised into a common
frame, reconciled geographically by a layer that is useful in its own right, and
served from archived copies with persistent identifiers rather than from
upstream URLs that no longer resolve. A figure costs two lines; the underlying
frame is one call away, so the framework is a point of entry rather than a
boundary.

The pandemic's open data are now a historical record, and an increasingly
fragile one, while the diseases that followed produce their own. Work ahead
follows from that: widening the catalogue beyond the mpox, measles and Ebola
series it already carries, deepening the
sub-national geolocation layer, and continuing to mirror sources before they
disappear. Contributions ---
in particular new database descriptors --- are welcome through the
repository~\cite{pyvoa_github}, which documents the procedure.

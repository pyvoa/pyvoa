\begin{abstract}

pyvoa (Python Virus Open Analysis) is an open-source Python library providing
access to 25 epidemiological data resources through a single query with five keywords. They range from global aggregators, such as Our World in Data, to
national agencies, and cover COVID-19, mpox, measles, and Ebola. Each resource
is described by a JSON file rather than by code, so the catalogue can be
extended without modifying the library. Resources are normalised into a common
pandas DataFrame, and a geolocation layer reconciles location identifiers and
provides geometries. Data are read from the project's Zenodo archive by
default, or directly from live sources for ongoing outbreaks, so series remain accessible after providers stop publishing. Charts and maps can be produced in a few lines of Python. pyvoa is MIT-licensed and available from PyPI.

\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and significance}
\label{sec:motivation}

The COVID-19 pandemic produced open epidemiological data at an unprecedented
scale. Global aggregators such as the Johns Hopkins University Center for
Systems Science and Engineering (JHU CSSE)~\cite{dong2020,dong2022} and Our
World in Data (OWID)~\cite{mathieu2021}, as well as national agencies such as
Santé publique France, the German Robert Koch-Institut, and the Italian
Dipartimento della Protezione Civile, published frequently updated series
online. These data, however, were released using heterogeneous file formats,
column vocabularies, temporal granularities and, above all, incompatible
geographic identifiers.

Two major challenges limit the reuse of these data: heterogeneity and
impermanence.

\bigskip

\textbf{Heterogeneity.} Datasets from different providers cannot generally be
compared directly. Equivalent indicators carry different column names from one
source to another, dates follow different formats, daily and cumulative
quantities appear under similar labels, country names vary
(\texttt{United States} / \texttt{US} / \texttt{USA}), and sub-national units
are identified sometimes by name and sometimes by code. 
Reporting schedules also differ: pauses over weekends or holidays can produce
artificial spikes when publication resumes~\cite{dong2022}. Comparative analysis therefore requires
source-specific reconciliation procedures, often implemented separately for
each study and not always made available alongside the resulting analysis. This
process is both time-consuming and error-prone. Heterogeneity also raises the
technical barrier for audiences to whom these data are particularly valuable:
secondary-school and university students, science journalists, and researchers
from other disciplines.



\bigskip

\textbf{Impermanence.} Open data are not necessarily permanent data. The JHU
CSSE team documented the substantial effort required to maintain access to
pandemic data~\cite{dong2022}: web scrapers were developed for more than four
hundred sources, each requiring maintenance whenever a provider changed the
structure of its website. Availability also changed over time. Some providers reduced the geographic resolution of their reporting; others
shifted from daily to weekly updates or adopted an irregular schedule. In other cases,
datasets themselves disappeared. The Chilean Ministry of Science, for
instance, published national COVID-19 data in a public GitHub repository that
is no longer publicly accessible~\cite{minciencia}.
Retrospective and comparative analyses depend on datasets that are sometimes
no longer available at their original locations and therefore cannot always be
independently retrieved by a reviewer. Long-term data accessibility has thus
become a key requirement for reproducibility.

\bigskip

Dashboards were an early response to the demand for accessible pandemic
information. A survey conducted in 2020 initially identified 265 public
web-based COVID-19 dashboards, of which 158 were ultimately included in its
comparative assessment~\cite{ivankovic2021}. Dashboards, however, are primarily
designed to display information rather than to provide reusable data. Their
choice of indicators, geographic coverage, and temporal scope is determined by
their authors; the underlying datasets may not be available for download; and
the displayed information cannot be readily recombined with data
from other sources.

\bigskip

Several software tools have also been developed to facilitate access to pandemic data. \texttt{covid}~\cite{covid_pypi} and
\texttt{COVID19Py}~\cite{covid19py} provide lightweight interfaces to a small
number of original data sources, but offer limited support for analysis and
visualisation.  The COVIDcast Epidata API~\cite{reinhart2021} offers a
well-documented collection of more than 170 signals from 12 data sources,
combining traditional public health indicators with auxiliary signals derived
from medical claims, surveys, mobility data, and internet search trends. These
signals are provided in a common format at fine geographic and temporal
resolution, and historical revisions are explicitly tracked. Its geographic
coverage, however, remains focused on the United States.
\texttt{covid19.analytics}~\cite{ponce2020} gives access within the R
ecosystem to several sources of global COVID-19 data, including case, testing,
vaccination, and genomic data. It also includes data-integrity checks,
analytical and modelling tools, and static and interactive visualisations.
Replicas of live datasets are maintained in a dedicated GitHub backup
repository for redundancy and robust accessibility.


\texttt{pyvoa} was designed to combine data access, geographic reconciliation,
visualisation, and data preservation within a single Python framework.
Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarises the main differences between
\texttt{pyvoa} and the tools discussed above.

The two challenges identified above are addressed in \texttt{pyvoa} through a
common architecture. Twenty-five data resources are described declaratively,
normalised into a common schema, and linked to a geographic layer that
reconciles location identifiers and provides the corresponding geometries.
Data can be retrieved either from their original sources, when current values
are required, or from the project's Zenodo deposits, which preserve previously
retrieved datasets. Once a data resource has been integrated, the same interface can
be used for data retrieval, geographic processing, and visualisation, allowing
a complete figure to be produced in only a few lines of Python.


\bigskip

This architecture is not specific to COVID-19. The catalogue has progressively
been extended to other infectious diseases and surveillance systems, including
mpox~\cite{owid_mpox}, acute respiratory infections monitored by the French
Réseau Sentinelles~\cite{sentinelles_ira}, measles cases in the United
States~\cite{ahmadi2025}, and the 2026 Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the
Democratic Republic of the Congo~\cite{bdbv2026}. These resources are described using the same JSON-based mechanism as their
COVID-19 counterparts. New resources can therefore be added without modifying
the core library.


\bigskip

The archival component complements this source-agnostic architecture by
preserving retrieved datasets as persistent and independently citable records.
Archived copies can therefore remain accessible even if the original source
changes or disappears, improving both recoverability and reproducibility.

\section{Software description}
\label{sec:description}

\subsection{Software architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

pyvoa is layered (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}). A user calls the front end; the
front end never touches a URL.

\textbf{Declarative source descriptions.} Every database is a JSON file in
\texttt{pyvoa/data/}. It carries the payload URL, the upstream reference URL,
the separator and decimal conventions, the columns to drop, the rows to select,
and a list of column descriptors mapping the source's own names onto pyvoa's
vocabulary --- with, for each, whether the series is cumulative and a
human-readable description. It also carries the geographic block: granularity
(\texttt{country}, \texttt{region}, \texttt{subregion}), the ISO~3166-1 alpha-3
code of the covered territory, and the location mode. \texttt{MetaInfo} and
\texttt{DataParser} interpret these files, so adding a source usually means
adding one JSON file and no Python at all.

\textbf{Geolocation.} Four classes handle geography. \texttt{GeoManager}
converts location names between the \texttt{iso2}, \texttt{iso3}, \texttt{name}
and \texttt{num} standards and absorbs the idiosyncratic spellings of individual
databases; all names pass through a normalisation that strips accents, collapses
separators and folds case, so \texttt{'esp'}, \texttt{'spain'} and
\texttt{'SPAIN'} resolve alike, as do \texttt{"cote d'ivoire"} and
\texttt{"Côte d'Ivoire"}. \texttt{GeoRegion} resolves supranational
groupings --- the UN M49 regions, plus the European Union, G7, G8, G20, G77,
OECD, BRICS, the Commonwealth and eight African and Latin American economic
communities --- so that a whole grouping is a single \texttt{where} argument.
\texttt{GeoCountry} provides sub-national units and their geometries for the
countries covered at that granularity, in main, dense or exploded variants so
that overseas territories can be drawn or set aside. \texttt{GPDBuilder} then
joins the epidemiological frame to the geometries and returns a geopandas
object. Formally, the layer implements
%
\begin{equation}
G:\ (\textit{source\_id},\ \textit{raw\_name}) \longrightarrow
(\textit{iso\_code},\ \textit{display\_name},\ \textit{geometry}),
\label{eq:geomapping}
\end{equation}
%
applied on loading, so that changing database leaves geographic identifiers
unchanged. The layer is importable on its own and useful outside epidemiology.

\textbf{Front end and visualisation.} \texttt{pyvoa.front} exposes a singleton's
methods at module level, so that the API reads as plain functions. Plotting
backends --- matplotlib~\cite{hunter2007}, bokeh~\cite{bokeh} and
seaborn~\cite{waskom2021} --- are optional imports probed at run time: the
library returns data with none of them installed, and reports a missing backend
rather than failing on import.

\subsection{Software functionalities}
\label{sec:functionalities}

\textbf{One query grammar.} Four functions --- \texttt{get()},
\texttt{plot()}, \texttt{map()} and \texttt{hist()} --- take the same five
keywords, after \texttt{setwhom()} has selected a database and, for charts,
\texttt{setvis()} a backend:
\texttt{where} (one place, a list of places, or a grouping),
\texttt{which} (an indicator of the selected database),
\texttt{what} (\texttt{current}, \texttt{daily} or \texttt{weekly}),
\texttt{when} (a date or a \texttt{begin:end} range, \texttt{dd/mm/yyyy}), and
\texttt{option} (\texttt{sumall}, \texttt{nonneg}, \texttt{smooth7}, or
normalisation by population at six scales, combinable in a list).
\texttt{get()} additionally takes \texttt{output}, returning a pandas or
geopandas frame, a list, a dict or an array; \texttt{input} accepts an external
frame, so pyvoa charts data it did not fetch.

\textbf{Introspection.} Every vocabulary is enumerable at run time ---
\texttt{listwhom()}, \texttt{listwhich()}, \texttt{listwhere()},
\texttt{listwhat()}, \texttt{listoption()}, \texttt{listvis()},
\texttt{listoutput()} --- each returning a plain list, and \texttt{whattodo()}
returning the whole keyword-value matrix as a frame. Discovery needs no
documentation lookup, which matters for the teaching use described in
Section~\ref{sec:impact}.

\textbf{Charts.} \texttt{plot()} draws \texttt{date}, \texttt{compare},
\texttt{versus}, \texttt{spiral} and \texttt{yearly} views; \texttt{hist()}
draws histograms by location or by value, and pie charts; \texttt{map()} draws
choropleths over standard or dense geometries, with a date slider under bokeh.
Titles, legends, watermarks and \texttt{savefig()} behave identically across
backends, and \texttt{setbatch()} renders without opening a window.

\textbf{Caching, mirroring and live sources.} Downloads are cached under
\texttt{\textasciitilde/.cache/pyvoa.data\_<user>/} with CRC32 invalidation; a
file below 1000 characters is treated as a failed download and re-fetched.
Since v0.5.0 the payloads themselves are read from the project's Zenodo
deposits, the upstream URL being retained in the descriptor. A series therefore
stays fetchable, and an analysis re-runnable, after its provider stops
publishing. That upstream URL is not merely documentary:
\texttt{setlive(True)} reads it instead of the mirror, which is what an
epidemic still under way requires, at the cost of the reproducibility the
archive gives. Of the twenty-five databases, twenty are readable both ways,
three only from the archive --- their provider has withdrawn the files --- and
two, added since the last deposit, only from their provider. The two copies are
cached apart, and a database absent from the archive falls back to its provider
in either mode.

\textbf{Errors.} \texttt{PyvoaError} is the library's single exception type; it
renders a coloured banner at construction, so the message reaches a notebook
user even when the traceback is hidden. Verbosity has three levels.

\subsection{Development history and quality assurance}
\label{sec:history}

pyvoa is the third name of one continuous project, developed throughout by the
same authors. \emph{CoCoA} (Covid Collaborative Analysis) began at the Data
Against COVID-19 hackathon of April 2020 with the aim it still has: unified
access to pandemic data for people who are not data specialists. It was renamed
\emph{PyCoA} (Python Covid Analysis) in November 2020 and developed under that
name until March 2025, accumulating some 1900 commits --- the multi-database
front end and the \texttt{GeoCountry} class in 2021, normalisation by
population, yearly and spiral plots in 2022, the JSON database descriptors in
2023, the matplotlib and seaborn backends in 2024. In early 2025 the
COVID-specific vocabulary was removed from the code base and the project became
\emph{pyvoa}, Python Virus Open Analysis: the point at which it stopped being
about one virus.

Version 0.5.0 (August 2026) is the first release prepared for external scrutiny.
The catalogue grew from 12 to 25 databases; payloads moved to the Zenodo
mirrors; packaging moved to PEP~621; an offline test suite was added, in which
any test needing an upstream server must be marked and is deselected by default,
so that a test cannot silently start depending on the network; and continuous
integration on GitHub Actions runs linting, the suite on Python 3.10 to 3.13,
and the network tests on a weekly schedule. Releases are published on PyPI and
archived on Zenodo with a concept DOI. The repository carries
\texttt{CITATION.cff}, \texttt{codemeta.json}, \texttt{.zenodo.json},
contribution and conduct guidelines, and a changelog covering every release
since 0.1.0 as well as the CoCoA and PyCoA years --- the only public record of a
history whose middle repository is private.

\section{Illustrative examples}
\label{sec:examples}

The four examples below are drawn from the notebook shipped with the package
and run in a plain Jupyter session after \texttt{pip install pyvoa-full}.

\subsection*{Example 1 --- comparing countries}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
import pyvoa.front as pf
pf.setwhom('owid')            # Our World in Data, worldwide, by country
pf.setvis('matplotlib')
pf.plot(which='total_deaths', where='Western Europe',
        what='daily', option='smooth7')
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{where='Western Europe'} expands through \texttt{GeoRegion} to the nine
states of the United Nations geoscheme subregion --- Switzerland,
Liechtenstein and Monaco among them, so the grouping is not the European Union
and does not have to be; \texttt{what='daily'} differentiates the cumulative
series the source publishes; \texttt{option='smooth7'} applies a weekly rolling
mean, removing the reporting-day artefact visible in every national series
(Fig.~\ref{fig:timeseries}).

\begin{figure}[!h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig2_timeseries_weu.pdf}
\caption{Example 1. Daily COVID-19 deaths in the nine Western European states,
seven-day smoothed, from Our World in Data.}
\label{fig:timeseries}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Example 2 --- mapping a grouping}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('jhu')             # Johns Hopkins CSSE, worldwide
pf.map(which='tot_confirmed', where='G20',
       what='daily', when='31/12/2021')
\end{lstlisting}

One keyword selects the G20 member states, and the geolocation layer supplies
their geometries; the countries outside the grouping are left unpainted. The
JHU series is read from its Zenodo mirror, the upstream collection having ended
in March 2023 (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}).

\begin{figure}[!h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig3_map_g20.pdf}
\caption{Example 2. Daily confirmed COVID-19 cases across the G20 member states
on 31~December 2021, from the Johns Hopkins CSSE mirror.}
\label{fig:map}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Example 3 --- sub-national data, normalised by population}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('spf')    # Sante Publique France db
pf.hist(which='cur_hosp',when='31/12/2021',option='normalize:pop1M')
\end{lstlisting}

The same keywords address a French source at \emph{département} granularity.
\texttt{option='normalize:pop1M'} divides the count by the population the
geolocation layer carries, turning patient numbers into a rate per million
inhabitants, which is what makes \emph{départements} of very different sizes
comparable at all; the bars are ranked by value rather than by name
(Fig.~\ref{fig:hist}). The same selection is returned as a pandas
frame~\cite{mckinney2010} by \texttt{pf.get()}, one row per date and place with
standardised geographic keys: pyvoa is a starting point for an analysis, not an
enclosure.

\begin{figure}[!h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig4_hist_spf.pdf}
\caption{Example 3. Patients hospitalised for COVID-19 per million inhabitants
by French \emph{département} on 31~December 2021, ranked, from Santé publique
France.}
\label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Example 4 --- beyond COVID-19}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=python]
pf.setwhom('ebolardc')
pf.hist(which='tot_confirmed',typeofhist='pie')
\end{lstlisting}

The grammar does not change with the disease. The situation reports of the
Institut National de Santé Publique are indexed by \emph{zone de santé}, a
geography the geolocation layer resolves --- and whose provinces it maps to
their ISO~3166-2 codes --- exactly as it does French \emph{départements} or
United States states~\cite{bdbv2026}; \texttt{typeofhist='pie'} asks for a
composition rather than a ranking, the smaller zones being collected into a
single slice instead of dropped (Fig.~\ref{fig:beyond}). Measles in the United
States~\cite{ahmadi2025}, mpox and the Réseau Sentinelles series for acute
respiratory infections are read on the same terms.

\begin{figure}[!h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/fig5_ebola_drc.pdf}
\caption{Example 4. Confirmed cases of the 2026 Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak
by health zone of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, from the situation
reports of the Institut National de Santé Publique.}
\label{fig:beyond}
\end{figure}

\section{Impact}
\label{sec:impact}

\textbf{Questions that become tractable.} Two kinds. First, cross-source
comparison. Because twenty-five databases share one schema and one geographic
key, a sub-national national source and an international country-level source
can be put side by side without writing a join: the French wastewater signal and
the reported incidence over the same territory, or a country's own agency series
against the aggregator's rendering of it --- a comparison that surfaces
reporting artefacts rather than assuming them away. Second, retrospective work
on sources that have ceased. The JHU series used in Section~\ref{sec:examples}
is no longer published; it is nonetheless fetched, by identifier, in one line.
The reverse case is served by the same code: an outbreak still under way, such
as the Ebola epidemic of Example~5, is read from the provider itself, the
archive being consulted only when it holds the file.

\textbf{Existing questions pursued better.} The gain is on reproducibility
rather than on speed. An analysis written against pyvoa records which database,
which indicator, which pre-treatment and which options were used, in five
keywords that a reader can re-execute; and because the payload is read from an
archived copy carrying a persistent identifier~\cite{zenodo_pyvoa} rather than
from a live URL, re-execution years later returns the same numbers. This is what
a live endpoint cannot offer, and it is increasingly the limiting factor in
pandemic retrospectives.

\textbf{Daily practice, and who changes it.} The design constraint --- a
complete figure in two lines, every vocabulary enumerable at run time --- was
set for teaching, and the software has been used that way at Université Paris
Cité since 2020, in physics and data analysis courses. Students meet real,
heterogeneous, incomplete data on the first day rather than a cleaned teaching
set; \texttt{get()} then hands them a pandas frame, so the progression from
first figure to independent analysis happens without changing tool. The same
property serves science journalists and researchers from outside epidemiology,
the other audiences the project has targeted from the start.

\textbf{Uptake so far.} The project began at the Data Against COVID-19 hackathon
(April 2020) and was presented at Hackathon COVID --- Lutter Ensemble (April
2021) and at the 22nd Salon Culture et Jeux Mathématiques (May 2021). It was
covered by the CNRS MODCOV19 modelling platform~\cite{modcov19} and in
institutional communications of Sorbonne Université, Université Paris Cité and
LPNHE. \attn{compléter ici par les usages tiers documentés --- voir note}

\textbf{Reuse, and commercial status.} The geolocation layer is importable on
its own (\texttt{pyvoa.geo}) and contains nothing epidemiological: any dataset
keyed by country, region or French \emph{département} can be reconciled and
mapped with it. Extending the catalogue takes a JSON descriptor and no Python,
which is what makes a new disease a matter of hours rather than of a release
cycle. The software is distributed under the MIT licence, has no commercial
version and is not exploited commercially.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

pyvoa gives one Python interface to twenty-five open epidemiological databases.
Sources are described declaratively rather than coded, normalised into a common
frame, reconciled geographically by a layer that is useful in its own right, and
served from archived copies with persistent identifiers rather than from
upstream URLs that no longer resolve. A figure costs two lines; the underlying
frame is one call away, so the framework is a point of entry rather than a
boundary.

The pandemic's open data are now a historical record, and an increasingly
fragile one, while the diseases that followed produce their own. Work ahead
follows from that: widening the catalogue beyond the mpox, measles, Ebola and
acute respiratory infection series it already carries, deepening the
sub-national geolocation layer, and continuing to mirror sources before they
disappear. Contributions ---
in particular new database descriptors --- are welcome through the
repository~\cite{pyvoa_github}, which documents the procedure.
